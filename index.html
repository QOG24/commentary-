The development of digital usage of nonprofit social welfare organisations

When solidarity
goes online.

Designing Instagram posts, administering a WhatsApp group, coordinating Zoom meetings…these are not activities that come first in mind when thinking about volunteer work, right? Yet, it is what more and more people end up spending their time doing when joining charities, especially students. This gap between why people join charities—usually wanting to help impoverished persons directly with on-the-ground actions—and what they actually do in them—digital activities— can lead to acute feelings of disconnection and disappointment, deteriorating one’s willingness to engage in social work; this paradox, related to the digitalisation of the workplace, is the core issue that underpins our study.

Research question

How has the use of digital tools reshaped and impacted nonprofit social welfare organisations, focusing on Sciences Po Menton campus’ societies landscape?

This interrogation will lead us to delve into the complex reconfigurations these organisations have been undergoing with the advent of the digital world at the turn of the 21st century.

Working hypotheses

Linked to broader sociological concepts of managerialisation and marketisation of the workplace, we will focus our attention on how this digital turn has impacted the solidarity sector and its goods and services—which have long been regarded as lying outside the market logic.

Our hypothesis will be twofold:

It will demonstrate the detrimental effects of digital tools on social work experience:

01

The paradox

There is a crucial paradox: nonprofit social welfare organisations’ mission is to counteract the harms of the market logic, yet, they end up importing that same logic through the tools they use—being built by and for corporate efficiency, they carry a commodifying rationality

Eg: Customer Relationship Management software (CRM)—initially built for sales teams—are sometimes used to "track engagement" and suddenly beneficiaries and volunteers are treated as a record to be tracked and optimized

02

The costs

Social organisations are put under pressure for efficiency and thereby adopt digital tools, yet, it comes with substantial costs to their ethics:

Eg: environmental costs (water/energy use of AI systems) and privacy costs (beneficiary data on external, marketing-oriented platforms) while claiming to be part of a values-driven, non-exploitative sector

Social workers are heavily impacted by these transformations: they can experience a sense of alienation, in the Marxist acceptation, and thus lose the solidarity spirit that got them to engage in the first place

Eg: the more work shifts are dedicated to digital tools instead of face-to-face outreach, the more they might disconnect from the person their labor is supposed to help

03

The nuance

However, our analysis would not be complete without mentioning the nuanced and sometimes beneficial aspects of this digital revolution:

Digital tools can lead to an enhanced efficiency, allowing for a greater number of possible beneficiaries, lowering barriers to entry for volunteer recruitment, and leading to a better access to information

These new technologies also help produce impact data, and encourage accountability through recipients’ feedbacks

Text analysis

Clicktivism,
slacktivism and connective action

Dr Max HALUPKA · 2022

Presentation

"Clicktivism, slacktivism and connective action" is a paper written by Dr Max HALUPKA (put file attached next to this), Senior Lecturer at the University of Canberra's School of Government and expert on the relationship between technology and politics. It was published in 2022, under part I out of 5, as part of The Elgar Encyclopedia of Technology and Politics. The latter offers a comprehensive overview of the ways in which technological development is reshaping politics by investigating the existing academic literature on the subject.

Field: intersection of political science, media studies, and political sociology

Method: The author maps the literature on the subject of online forms of political participation in order to comprehend how it can be categorised and understood, to eventually assess its scope and usefulness

Angle: Very descriptive and analytical; there is no specific thesis argued, except that online “activism” political legitimacy relies on which definition is used

Main arguments

The paper’s main aim is to define three different ways of regarding online digital political participation:

01

Clicktivism

Clicktivism (seen as politically legitimate) = “a type of digital political participation that is noncommittal, nonspecialized and easily replicated, and that engages a political object” (Halupka, 2014) → it refers to an impulsive mechanism of political expression which is nowadays well entrenched in “citizen”s participatory repertoire”

It is facilitated by the ever-increasing digitalisation of our lives—ie, “the proliferation of sociotechnical environments, such as social media”(Karpf,2020)

It helps connect individual actions and collective movements, such that it challenges traditional forms of political participation

7 characteristics: Digitally situated, Impulsive, Noncommittal, Does not draw upon specialised knowledge, Easily replicated, Engages a political Object (i.e., an existing, broader political content)

HOWEVER it is only “a low-risk, low-cost activity” that is MORE focused on personal self-satisfaction rather than actually impactful, tangible actions

It links with the concept of “performative activism”(eg, signing a petition or liking & sharing a post rather than getting involved in a political party, participating in a protest…)

02

Slacktivism

Slacktivism (seen as a non-legitimate political action): this concept is a critique that derides the emergence of clicktivism

It highlights the inefficacy & egoism of clicktivist behaviours

YET, it shows that it is detrimental BECAUSE it moves people away from on-the-ground, collective political engagement by making them think clicktivism has an impact; it leads to a widespread “political complacency” and hinders genuine political consciousness to emerge

Is only relevant in the social fields (Bourdieu) that already understand and accept clicktivism as legitimate and it relies on an open democratic form of government

03

Connective action

Connective action: a theory seeing online activism (like clicktivism) as a legitimate organising principle

It posits that “communication functions as organisation” = allows for collective actions to occur (it enables to create relationships, transfer information, etc)

It argues that it brings more horizontal forms of political engagement—political actions therefore decouple from traditional political authorities and institutions

BUT it is only a “thin community” based on common concerns SO it is more fragile and fragmented than the traditional ones based on ideologies (eg, only being in a charity supporting exiled people BUT not in a political party that fights capitalism, neocolonialism, imperialism and all the root causes of forced migration)

The main distinction:

Agent-based definitions = clicktivism and connective action

They find political legitimacy in online activism because they posit that legitimacy comes from what is produced/”clicked” by the agent

BUT the problem = agents are likely to miss that specific struggles are all embedded in larger ideologies, leading to detrimental fragmentation of political consciousness and collective action

Structure-based definition = slacktivism

It finds no legitimacy in online activism because it sees political legitimacy as only emerging from broader collective actions and ideologies and not individual online “clicks”

Text analysis

Key takeaways for our study

The concepts and debate on digital activism’s political legitimacy in Max HALUPKA’s paper is very much linked to our research question; it gives us relevant frameworks to use when conducting our study.

In fact, volunteer work ought to be regarded as a political act: when people get involved in nonprofit social welfare organisations, they are moved by a specific societal issue, and, by acting on it, they express a political sentiment and desire.

Yet, what happens when organisations that were traditionally based on face-to-face relationships become heavily dependent on virtual networks? Can these digital technologies even replace human interaction, or prompt the creation of online organisations, with very little infrastructural organisation and on-the-ground actions?

These interrogations are compelling with regards to our Sciences Po Menton case study. Most student societies, notably the ones that directly aim to support vulnerable communities (e.g., SPRH: SPRH Menton), are faced with a striking paradox, Menton’s demographics: a peaceful small city with an aging population (above the national average rate: Menton demographics) and where executives and mid-career professionals represent more than a quarter of the population, the vulnerable communities targeted by student societies are not so much present.

Therefore, the biggest part of volunteer time is dedicated to digital activities (organising meetings, allocating work on Google Sheets, reaching out to possible speakers for conferences, writing awareness-raising materials on a laptop, etc).

Additionally, a lot of students engage in “slacktivism”, i.e., sharing posts on social media and writing papers but not participating in protests or donating time and money to bigger charities—this could be explained, for example, by a fear of repercussions from their university or a disinterest for more concrete, non-digital political actions.

Moreover, most students are from privileged background—less than 3% of students admitted in 2026 were from the Convention d’Education Prioritaire (CEP) pathway (Sciences Po admissions)— explaining why such a small level of political engagement can feel sufficient.

In our group project, we will therefore assess the relevance of the concepts of “Clicktivism, slacktivism and connective action” to our case study, to see if digital tools have led to student societies to lose a little bit of their political relevance and impact.
